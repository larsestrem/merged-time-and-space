Work With Us

The classroom pages are being updated. We are making an update to the classroom and lesson-plan pages, expected in October or November. Lesson-plan submissions are paused until then — please check back.

Every tool here is unfinished in some way, and the people best placed to say how are the ones using it in a room full of eleven-year-olds. Tell us what a tool should do next. If we build your class's idea, your class is credited on the page. It costs nothing, it never involves student data, and you can stop at any point.

Three ways in

A class sends ideasStudents explore a tool and say what would make it better, clearer, more accurate or more fun. The teacher sends the list. Anything we build carries the class's name.
A teacher brings a lessonYou tell us what you're teaching and how you'd use a tool. We fix what gets in the way and write the lesson plan with you, then publish it free for other teachers.
Anyone tells us what's wrongA wrong number, a confusing label, something that breaks on a school Chromebook, a page a screen reader can't get through. Criticism is the cheapest useful thing you can send us.

You do not need to be a teacher. Homeschool parents, librarians, museum and planetarium staff, science-club leaders, and people who simply use the site and have a better idea are all equally welcome — the pages have to work for whoever opens them.

What happens after you write

  • 1 · You send it. The class request form is the easiest route — what you're teaching, the grade or year group, and what you'd like the tool to do. Rough is fine. "The moon page is too much at once for my third graders" is a real, useful message.
  • 2 · A person reads it. Not a queue, not a bot. You get a reply, including when the answer is "that one is beyond what this site can honestly compute".
  • 3 · We work out what it really is. Most good ideas arrive as a wish and leave as a specific change. That conversation is the valuable part, and it usually improves the idea.
  • 4 · We build it. Small things can be live the same week; a new simulator takes longer. You'll know which one it is before we start.
  • 5 · You see it before anyone else does. If it came from your class, you get to tell us it isn't right yet. That is not a formality — a tool that misses the point in a classroom is a tool that failed.
  • 6 · The credit goes up. On the page itself, in the same words you approved — and carried across if that page is ever rebuilt.

The credit, exactly

If your class's idea gets built, a line like this goes on the tool:

This simulator was inspired by an idea from Mr. Smith's 5th grade class at Paradise Elementary School in Paradise, California.

Real school, real class — mine. Paradise Elementary in Paradise, California is where I went, and Mr. Smith taught my fifth grade class there more than thirty years ago. No class has asked for anything yet, so that line is still only an example of the format; it seemed like the right name to hold the place until a real one takes it.

Imagine being in fifth grade, having an idea, and later finding your class named on a tool that students on the other side of the world are using. That is the whole point of doing it this way. Children who are told their ideas can become real things behave differently towards the next idea they have.

What is published

The teacher's nameAs you write it — "Mr. Smith", "Ms. Smith's class", a first initial, or nothing at all.
The grade or year group"5th grade", "Year 6", "a combined 3rd/4th".
The town and regionAnd the school year, if you want it.
The school's nameOnly if you ask for it. Left out by default.

What is never published

Nothing about a student. Ever. Not a name, not a photograph, not a piece of work, not an email address. Please don't send them — we don't want them, we don't need them, and we delete them if they arrive. The credit is opt-in, and if you change your mind a year later, ask on the report page and it comes down. The whole policy is in the privacy policy and the terms.

Ideas that are easy to say yes to

The site's one hard rule is that everything on it is computed from real motions and real measurements, never typed in from memory and never guessed. That rule decides what can be built, so it is worth knowing before you send an idea. Things that fit:

  • "Show it for our town." Anything the sun, moon and tide pages do for a city, they can do for yours.
  • "Show it for a date that matters to us." A birthday, a field trip, the day of the eclipse, the first day of school.
  • "Make it simpler for younger children." Fewer numbers, bigger type, one idea per screen. Several pages need this and we know it.
  • "Let us change something and see what happens." Speeds, distances, dates, tilt. The strongest ideas we've had are all of this shape.
  • "Put the two things side by side." Two cities, two dates, two planets. Comparison is where most understanding actually happens.
  • "We need it to survive our school network." No sign-in, no plugin, works on a locked-down Chromebook, prints on paper.

And the honest limits: we won't build anything that needs student accounts or collects data about children, anything that depends on a paid data feed we can't keep alive, or anything that would require the site to state as fact something it cannot actually work out. When an idea runs into one of those, we'll say so and explain why, rather than quietly not doing it.

What we can and can't promise

Free to youThere is nothing to pay for using the tools or for anything we build together, and no account or sign-up to get at it. The site carries no advertising and sells nothing today; if it ever needs to pay for itself, the standard that would have to meet (and what a sponsor could and could not buy) is written down, and it starts with the tools staying free and nothing tracking a student.
Every message readBy a person, with a reply. Including the ones that tell us something is wrong.
Not every idea builtWe can't promise any particular idea gets made, or a date it would be done by. Don't plan a lesson around something that doesn't exist yet — plan it around what's on the site today, and tell us what was missing afterwards.
Credit where it's earnedIf your idea shaped what we built, your class is named for it. If several classes shaped it, they are all named.
Your school's rules come firstPlease check you're allowed to contact an outside site before you write. We'd rather you asked and then wrote.
