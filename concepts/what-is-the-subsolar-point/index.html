Subsolar point

What is the subsolar point?

It is the one place on Earth where the Sun is straight overhead right now — where a flagpole casts no shadow. It is the yellow dot on the day/night map, and it is where the Sun-centre to Earth-centre line hits the surface.

A lap a day, a bounce a year

Earth turns. The overhead point therefore races west, once around the planet each day, at the speed of the ground under it — about 1,670 km/h at the equator.

It also drifts north and south with the seasons, because of the tilt. In June it reaches the Tropic of Cancer. In December it reaches the Tropic of Capricorn. It never leaves that band.

How this page finds it

The map does not look up a table of “where the Sun is.” It solves a low-precision solar series (the same one used for sunrise on this site): the Sun’s apparent ecliptic longitude, the obliquity, then declination and right ascension. Declination is the latitude of the subsolar point. Greenwich sidereal time turns right ascension into longitude.

That is why the yellow dot and the side-view hit are the same number. One series, two pictures.

Related questions

What is the Tropic of Cancer? The Tropic of Cancer is the circle at 23.4° north. On one day each year — the summer solstice, around June 21 — the Sun stands straight overhead on this line. North of it, the Sun is never straight overhead.

What is the terminator? The terminator is the line between day and night — the circle on Earth where the Sun sits on the horizon. Sunrise is where you cross it going into the light; sunset is where you cross it going out.

What is Earth’s axial tilt? Earth’s spin axis is not upright. It leans about 23.4° from the vertical to its orbit. That lean is why we have tropics, polar circles, and seasons — and it barely changes from one year to the next.
