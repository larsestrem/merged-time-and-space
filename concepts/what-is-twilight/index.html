Twilight

What is twilight?

Twilight is the time when the Sun is below the horizon but still lighting the sky. This map draws it as a soft band: from the terminator down to where the Sun is 18° below — the edge of astronomical night.

Three common edges

Civil twilight: Sun 0° to 6° down — streetlights, a clear horizon. Nautical: 6° to 12° — the brighter stars, a usable sea horizon. Astronomical: 12° to 18° — the sky is not yet fully dark for faint objects. Past 18° down, night.

This map uses the far edge, −18°, so the band you see is the whole of twilight, not just the civil slice.

Why summer nights stay pale

At mid-latitudes the Sun dives steeply. Twilight is short. Near the polar circles the Sun skims the horizon, so it takes hours to fall 18°. In June, much of the north never reaches astronomical night at all. That is a geometry fact, not a weather one.

Related questions

What is the terminator? The terminator is the line between day and night — the circle on Earth where the Sun sits on the horizon. Sunrise is where you cross it going into the light; sunset is where you cross it going out.

What are the polar circles? The Arctic Circle and Antarctic Circle sit at 66.6° north and south — 90° minus Earth’s tilt. Inside them the Sun can stay up all day in summer, or stay down all day in winter.

Why is this map flat? This map gives every degree of latitude and longitude the same length on the page, so meridians are straight and the terminator is honest. The cost is size: land near the poles is stretched. The picture says so, in numbers from the drawing.
