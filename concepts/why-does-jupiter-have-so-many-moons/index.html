Jupiter’s moons

Why does Jupiter have so many moons?

Gravity’s reach grows with mass, and Jupiter has more of it than anything in the solar system except the Sun. A wide region around it is a place where a small body stays with Jupiter instead of orbiting the Sun on its own.

A planet that acts like a small system

The same falling-and-missing that keeps Earth beside the Sun keeps Io beside Jupiter. More mass means a stronger pull at any distance, and so a larger volume of space where “beside Jupiter” beats “beside the Sun.” Jupiter has more mass than every other planet combined — its keep-a-moon region is correspondingly enormous.

Four worlds you can see for yourself

Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto formed from a disc around young Jupiter, the way the planets formed from a disc around the Sun — a solar system in miniature. Ganymede is bigger than the planet Mercury. All four show in steady binoculars as pinpricks in a line beside the planet, shifting night to night; watching them move is how Galileo proved not everything orbits Earth, and you can repeat the observation from a backyard.

Captured crowds and a moving count

Beyond the four giants, the census is mostly captured asteroids on distant, tilted, often backwards orbits — snatched from the belt Jupiter itself stirs. Counting “how many moons” is then a census of what telescopes can currently see, not a closed number, which is why the figure in the news keeps changing. Saturn’s count climbs for exactly the same reason.

Related questions

Why does Saturn have so many moons? Saturn holds the largest confirmed-moon count of any planet, and it keeps climbing as surveys find smaller ones. It is the second-heaviest planet, so an enormous region around it is one where a small icy body stays with Saturn instead of orbiting the Sun. A few large moons formed there; most of the crowd was captured later.

How does an orbit work? A planet is always falling toward the Sun, and it is already moving sideways. Gravity only pulls inward — it never pushes the planet along. Those two together bend a straight line into a closed curve. That closed path is the orbit.

Why don’t planets fall into the Sun? They are falling. They are also moving sideways. By the time a planet has fallen, the Sun is no longer where it was falling toward. Falling and missing, without end, is an orbit. There is no outward force holding them up.
