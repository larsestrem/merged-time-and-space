Giant-planet moons

Why does Saturn have so many moons?

Gravity’s reach grows with mass. Jupiter and Saturn are the two heaviest planets, so a huge volume of space around each one is a place where a small body stays with the planet instead of orbiting the Sun on its own. Some of those moons formed there. Many more were captured. See Jupiter’s moons and Saturn’s moons.

One giant, then a crowd

Titan is the moon that formed beside Saturn the way planets formed beside the Sun, and it holds almost all the mass that orbits the planet — a world bigger than Mercury, with weather and rivers of methane. After Titan come a handful of mid-sized icy moons, and then the census dissolves into hundreds of captured ice-rocks a few kilometres across, on long, tilted, often backwards orbits.

That is why the count keeps changing: the big moons were all found long ago, and what survey telescopes keep adding is the small captured crowd at the bottom of the list. “How many moons does Saturn have” is a census question, not a fixed fact — the honest answer always carries a date.

The rings are not failed moons on the list

Inside a certain distance of Saturn, its gravity stretches an orbiting body harder than the body’s own gravity can hold it together — so close in, there can be rings but no large moon. Saturn’s rings live inside that line. Right at its edge sit tiny shepherd moons, herding the ring edges sharp; they are on the moon list, while the countless ring particles they herd are not.

Why the rocky planets keep almost none

Earth has one large moon from a giant impact. Mars has two tiny ones. Mercury and Venus have none. Close to the Sun the region where a planet’s gravity beats the Sun’s is small, so there is little room to keep a captured body, and less leftover ice to build one. Mass and distance both decide the guest list — Saturn is heavy and far, so its list is the longest. Jupiter’s answer is the same physics with even more mass behind it.

Related questions

Why does Jupiter have so many moons? Gravity’s reach grows with mass, and Jupiter has more of it than anything in the solar system except the Sun. A wide region around it is a place where a small body stays with Jupiter instead of orbiting the Sun on its own.

Why do planets have moons, and how are they made? Three ways, and you can tell them apart. Some moons form from the same disc as their planet. Some form from the debris of a giant impact. Some are captured — they were going past and got stuck. Triton’s backward orbit is the capture giveaway. Earth’s Moon is the impact story.

Why doesn’t Earth have a ring? A ring is ice or rock that orbits inside a limit where a moon would be pulled apart. Earth could wear one if a moon broke up that close. Our Moon is too far out and too well stuck together. Saturn’s rings are the famous leftover — ice that never quite made a moon, or a moon that came too close.
