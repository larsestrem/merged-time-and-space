Day and Night Map

Where it is light on Earth right now, where it is dark, and the twilight in between. Drag the slider to move through one 27.3-day orbit of the Moon, or press play and watch the line sweep round. The same picture as the world clock's map, with time attached.

  

The sun is overhead at 9.2° N, 67.0° W — this is the subsolar point. This time of year the sun is moving from summer toward fall, and days in the northern half of the world are getting shorter because Earth is tilting away from the sun in Earth’s orbit.

Play runs at about six hours a second: one full turn of the Earth takes 4 seconds, and one 27.3-day orbit of the Moon about 2 minutes. Nothing on the map slides sideways while it runs — the shaded shape is re-solved for each moment, meridian by meridian — so the picture never runs off the edge and jumps back. The one thing that does cross the edge is the sun marker, and that is honest: this map is a cylinder cut open at the date line, so the point the sun stands over leaves at one side and arrives at the other.

What this is, and how it works

This is a live map of day and night on Earth, solved for this moment. Half the planet is in sunlight at every instant. The bright half is where the sun is above the horizon, the dark half is below, and the soft band is twilight — the sun has set but the sky is still lit, out to 18° down.

The yellow marker is the one place the sun stands straight overhead. The moon marker is where the moon stands overhead at the time on the slider, drawn in its current phase. It can sit in daylight or in night; that is a daytime moon. Drag the slider end to end and that marker completes one 27.3-day orbit of the Moon around Earth. The dashed gold lines are the tropics. The curve is the terminator. Play watches both markers sweep west. Jump to a season start to see the shadow lean.

Where the sun is standing, seen from the side

The map above looks down at the ground. This is the same instant from beside Earth’s orbit — parallel sunlight, and the one yellow line from the centre of the sun to the centre of the Earth. It meets the surface at the yellow marker.

That line lands at 9.2° N — 14.3° short of the Tropic of Cancer, which is as far north as it can ever get. The northern half of the world is leaning into the light, so more of it falls inside the lit half than outside, and its days are longer than its nights. That lean is the tilt of Earth on its orbit.

The two dashed chords are the tropics, at ±23.4°. They are the tilt written on the surface. Jump the map to a solstice and watch the yellow line stop there.

Things to try

  • Find your own bedtime. The map marks where you are as soon as your browser shares it — if it did not, there is a pin in the season-button row. Then drag the slider to tonight and watch the shading arrive over your dot: that is your sunset, to the minute the sun goes down where you are standing.
  • Watch the moon go round. Drag the slider from one end to the other. That is 27.3 days — one real orbit of the Moon around Earth — and the moon marker comes back to nearly the same place it started. The phase will not match: a full cycle of phases takes two days longer.
  • Who is asleep right now? Press Now and look at which continents sit in the dark half. Then pick a country the class has a connection to and check whether anyone there would answer the phone.
  • Race the line. Press Play and follow the terminator west. It crosses the whole map in 24 hours, which at the equator is about 1,670 km/h — faster than an airliner. Ask which way you would have to fly to keep the sun from setting.
  • Break the tilt. Jump to the summer solstice, then to the winter solstice, and watch the top of the map swap from all-light to all-dark. Ask what would happen to seasons if the tilt were zero — the answer is on the map, because the line would simply stand up straight.
  • Catch the equinox. Jump to the spring equinox or the fall equinox and check the line: nearly vertical, and every place on Earth getting about twelve hours of each. It is the only date the map is symmetric.
  • Argue with the map. Compare Greenland with Africa, then look up their real areas. This is the cheapest possible lesson in why the projection matters, and it lands harder when the map is one they have just been using and trusting.

Questions this page answers

The question is the link. A short answer sits here; tap through for the drawing and the deeper pass.

  • What is the Tropic of Cancer? The Tropic of Cancer is the circle at 23.4° north.

  • What is the Tropic of Capricorn? The Tropic of Capricorn is the circle at 23.4° south.

  • What is the subsolar point? It is the one place on Earth where the Sun is straight overhead right now — where a flagpole casts no shadow.

  • What is the terminator? The terminator is the line between day and night — the circle on Earth where the Sun sits on the horizon.

  • What is Earth’s axial tilt? Earth’s spin axis is not upright.

  • What are the polar circles? The Arctic Circle and Antarctic Circle sit at 66.6° north and south — 90° minus Earth’s tilt.

  • What is twilight? Twilight is the time when the Sun is below the horizon but still lighting the sky.

  • Why is this map flat? This map gives every degree of latitude and longitude the same length on the page, so meridians are straight and the terminator is honest.

  • Why do we have seasons? Not because Earth gets closer to the Sun.

  • Can the moon be up in the daytime? Yes. The moon is above the horizon about half of every day, and those hours drift through daylight as the month goes by. It is simply bright enough to beat the blue sky. On the day/night map the pale marker is where the moon stands overhead — watch it ride through the lit half.

  • What is a time zone? A political stripe painted on a rotating ball.

  • What is a solstice? A solstice is a day the Sun reaches the farthest point it ever gets from the equator — 23.4° north around June 21, 23.4° south around December 21.

  • What is an equinox? An equinox is one of the two days a year the Sun stands directly over the equator — around March 20 and again around September 23.
