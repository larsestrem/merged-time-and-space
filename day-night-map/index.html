Nothing on the map slides sideways while it runs — the shaded shape is re-solved for each moment, meridian by meridian — so the picture never runs off the edge and jumps back. The one thing that does cross the edge is the sun marker, and that is honest: this map is a cylinder cut open at the date line, so the point the sun stands over leaves at one side and arrives at the other.

The start and stop of each season

Each marked Earth is the start of a season, and the stop of the one before. Spring starts at the spring equinox and runs until the summer solstice; summer, fall and winter follow the same way around. Every Earth leans the same 23.4° — that unmoving tilt is why we have seasons. The gold half is sunlight; the north end leans into it in June and out of it in December.

The yellow dot is today on the year. Press a season name, or tap an Earth, to jump the map to that start.

What this is, and how it works

This is a live map of day and night on Earth, solved for this moment. Half the planet is in sunlight at every instant. The bright half is where the sun is above the horizon, the dark half is below, and the soft band is twilight — the sun has set but the sky is still lit, out to 18° down.

The yellow marker is the one place the sun stands straight overhead. The moon marker is where the moon stands overhead at the time on the slider, drawn in its current phase. It can sit in daylight or in night; that is a daytime moon. The dashed gold lines are the tropics. The curve is the terminator. Play watches both markers sweep west. Where the sun is standing, seen from the side

The map above looks down at the ground. This is the same instant from beside Earth’s orbit — parallel sunlight, and the one yellow line from the centre of the sun to the centre of the Earth. It meets the surface at the yellow marker.

That line lands at 9.9° N — 13.5° short of the Tropic of Cancer, which is as far north as it can ever get. The northern half of the world is leaning into the light, so more of it falls inside the lit half than outside, and its days are longer than its nights. That lean is the tilt of Earth in Earth’s orbit.

The two dashed chords are the tropics, at ±23.4°. They are the tilt written on the surface. Jump the map to a solstice and watch the yellow line stop there.

Questions this page answers

The question is the link. A short answer sits here; tap through for the drawing and the deeper pass.

  • What is the Tropic of Cancer? The Tropic of Cancer is the circle at 23.4° north. On one day each year — the summer solstice, around June 21 — the Sun stands straight overhead on this line. North of it, the Sun is never straight overhead.

  • What is the Tropic of Capricorn? The Tropic of Capricorn is the circle at 23.4° south. On one day each year — the winter solstice, around December 21 — the Sun stands straight overhead on this line. South of it, the Sun is never straight overhead. It is the southern twin of the Tropic of Cancer.

  • What is the subsolar point? It is the one place on Earth where the Sun is straight overhead right now — where a flagpole casts no shadow. It is the yellow dot on the day/night map, and it is where the Sun-centre to Earth-centre line hits the surface.

  • What is the terminator? The terminator is the line between day and night — the circle on Earth where the Sun sits on the horizon. Sunrise is where you cross it going into the light; sunset is where you cross it going out.

  • What is Earth’s axial tilt? Earth’s spin axis is not upright. It leans about 23.4° from the vertical to its orbit. That lean is why we have tropics, polar circles, and seasons — and it barely changes from one year to the next.

  • What are the polar circles? The Arctic Circle and Antarctic Circle sit at 66.6° north and south — 90° minus Earth’s tilt. Inside them the Sun can stay up all day in summer, or stay down all day in winter.

  • What is twilight? Twilight is the time when the Sun is below the horizon but still lighting the sky. This map draws it as a soft band: from the terminator down to where the Sun is 18° below — the edge of astronomical night.

  • Why is this map flat? This map gives every degree of latitude and longitude the same length on the page, so meridians are straight and the terminator is honest. The cost is size: land near the poles is stretched. The picture says so, in numbers from the drawing.

  • Why do we have seasons? Not because Earth gets closer to the Sun. When it is summer in the United States it is winter in Australia, on the same planet, at the same distance. Seasons happen because the axis is tilted 23.4° and keeps pointing the same way all year.

  • Can the moon be up in the daytime? Yes. The moon is above the horizon about half of every day, and those hours drift through daylight as the month goes by. It is simply bright enough to beat the blue sky. On the day/night map the pale marker is where the moon stands overhead — watch it ride through the lit half.
