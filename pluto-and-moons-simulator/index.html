Pluto

Home/Planets/Pluto

A dwarf planet, a double world, and the only one of these anybody has visited exactly once. Where it is right now, how big and how heavy, what goes round it, and what is still unexplained.

Pluto on its axis

One turn takes6.4 days · backwards
Axis tilted122.53°
Its year248.1 Earth years
Turns per year14186.9

Turning here at one rotation every 12 seconds, which is 45,988 times real speed. What is not sped up: which way it turns — backwards, and that is real, how far the axis leans, and how its day compares with every other planet's. The face New Horizons saw in 2015. Sputnik Planitia — the western lobe of the heart — is a 1,000 km sheet of nitrogen ice and the brightest thing on the planet; Cthulhu Macula is the dark band along the equator beside it, stained red-brown by tholins. The other hemisphere is far less well known, and is drawn with far less on it.

The moons of Pluto, one at a time

The table above has every figure — how far out, how long a lap takes, how wide. What follows is what those figures cannot tell you: what each one is actually like, who found it and when, and what is still argued about it.

Charon

Half Pluto’s width. The two do not orbit each other so much as orbit a point in the empty space between them.

Across1,212 km
Distance from Pluto19,591 km
One orbit6.39 days
FoundJames Christy, 1978, as a bump that kept moving round the edge of Pluto on photographic plates
  • That balance point lies OUTSIDE Pluto’s surface, which is true of no planet-and-moon pair in the solar system. They are a genuine double system.
  • Both are tidally locked to each other: from one side of Pluto, Charon hangs motionless in the sky forever, and from the other it is never seen at all.
  • Its north pole is a deep red cap — methane escaping from Pluto, freezing out on the coldest ground on Charon, and being cooked by ultraviolet into tholins.
  • Serenity Chasma is part of a canyon system running about 1,800 km, four times the length of the Grand Canyon and twice as deep.

Still unsettled

The tectonics say Charon once had an ocean that froze and expanded, splitting the crust. Whether anything is left of it is unknown.

Nix

One of four small moons that tumble unpredictably, because they are orbiting a pair rather than a point.

Across50.0 km
Distance from Pluto48,694 km
One orbit24.9 days
FoundHubble Space Telescope, 2005
  • With two large bodies at the centre, the gravity field a small moon feels changes as the pair rotates, so its spin never settles. Nix and Hydra both tumble chaotically.
  • Nix would send its "day" from one length to another within a couple of orbits — sunrise on it is not a schedule, it is a surprise.

Pluto: things worth knowing

  • It is a dwarf planet, not a planet. The IAU drew the line in 2006: a planet has to have cleared its orbital neighbourhood, and Pluto shares the Kuiper belt with a great many other bodies.
  • Pluto and Charon are a double world. Charon is half Pluto's diameter and about an eighth of its mass, which puts the point they both orbit outside Pluto's surface — no other named pair here does that. They also keep the same faces to each other permanently.
  • Its orbit crosses Neptune's, and the two can never collide. Pluto goes round twice for every three Neptune orbits, a resonance that keeps them apart; the closest they ever get is about 17 AU.
  • It was inside Neptune's orbit from 1979 to 1999. For those twenty years Neptune, not Pluto, was the outermost planet.
  • Sputnik Planitia — the left half of the famous heart — is a basin of nitrogen ice that convects in cells tens of kilometres across, resurfacing itself far too fast for any crater to survive.
  • Only one spacecraft has ever been there. New Horizons flew past on 14 July 2015 after a nine-and-a-half-year crossing, and everything close-up that anyone knows about Pluto came from that single afternoon.
  • The four small moons tumble. Styx, Nix, Kerberos and Hydra do not keep a face to Pluto, because the gravity they sit in is a rotating pair rather than a point — their day lengths change unpredictably.

What we still don’t know about Pluto

Every one of these is genuinely unsettled — not simplified for the page, not waiting on a textbook update.

Is there an ocean under the ice?

Sputnik Planitia sits almost exactly on the line between Pluto and Charon, which is where a heavy patch would end up if the crust could slide — and a subsurface ocean is the most natural way to make that patch heavy. It is strong circumstantial evidence and not a measurement; nothing has sounded the interior.

Does the atmosphere freeze out completely as it moves away from the sun?

It was expected to collapse as Pluto receded past its 1989 perihelion, and instead the pressure rose for two decades before starting to fall. How much survives at aphelion, 49 AU out, has not been observed — nobody has watched Pluto through a full 248-year orbit.

How did it get five moons?

A giant impact that made Charon is the standard answer, with the small four condensing from the debris. It accounts for the mass ratio and the tidy, near-circular, coplanar orbits, but not for every detail of their sizes and spacings.

Recently learned about Pluto

2015–2016New Horizons returned the only close-up data there is: nitrogen-ice convection in Sputnik Planitia, mountains of water ice several kilometres high, and a layered haze reaching 200 km up that nobody had predicted.
2022Re-analysis of the flyby data put the surface of Sputnik Planitia at under about ten million years old, and probably far younger — a body that small was expected to be geologically dead.
