Where everything actually is, right now. Every planet, a page each, turning on its own axis with its moons going round it — the whole system on its real orbits, and the next launch window to Mars. The positions are solved when the page loads, not drawn from memory.

  • Why don’t planets fall into the Sun? They are falling. They are also moving sideways. By the time a planet has fallen, the Sun is no longer where it was falling toward. Falling and missing, without end, is an orbit. There is no outward force holding them up.

  • How does an orbit work? At every instant a planet has a velocity — where it is already going — and gravity adds a small pull straight toward the Sun. Bend a path by the same small amount at every step and it closes. That closed path is the orbit.

  • Why does Jupiter have so many moons? Gravity’s reach grows with mass, and Jupiter has more of it than anything in the solar system except the Sun. A wide region around it is a place where a small body stays with Jupiter instead of orbiting the Sun on its own.

What if a moon slowed down?

A circle: falling exactly as fast as the curve carries it away.

Nothing spirals in. Take speed away and the far side of the orbit drops toward the planet; add speed and it climbs away — and either way the moon comes back through the point where you changed it.
